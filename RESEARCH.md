# Which gratitude prompts work best?

This is the research behind the app's prompt library and its three starting prompts.

## The short version

- **Gratitude journaling helps, but only a little.** Meta-analyses find small effects on well-being. Against neutral control activities the effect is very small. Expect a gentle nudge, not a transformation.
- **How you write matters more than how much.** The best-supported prompts ask you to:
  - explain *why* a good thing happened
  - name a *person*, not a thing
  - go into detail about one item rather than list many
  - imagine the good thing *never having happened*
- **More often isn't better.** In one study, writing once a week raised happiness and writing three times a week did not. A likely cause is adaptation: the practice goes stale. So the app never nags, missing days are fine, and you can swap prompts any time.

## Evidence for each technique

| Technique | What the study found | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Three Good Things**: write three things that went well and *why* | Participants were happier than controls, an effect seen at 1 week, 1, 3 and 6 months. The sample was mostly white, educated Americans recruited online. | Seligman, Steen, Park & Peterson (2005), *American Psychologist* 60(5) |
| **Counting blessings** (weekly list of up to 5 things) | Compared with listing hassles or neutral events, people felt better about their lives, were more optimistic about the coming week and reported fewer physical symptoms. | Emmons & McCullough (2003), *JPSP* 84(2) |
| **Mental subtraction**: imagine a good thing never happened | Raised positive mood more than writing about how the good thing came about. People *predicted the opposite*, which is why it's a non-obvious prompt. Writing about how you might never have met your partner raised relationship satisfaction. Effects were modest. | Koo, Algoe, Wilson & Gilbert (2008), *JPSP* 95(5) |
| **Gratitude to people** / gratitude letters | Writing gratitude letters raised happiness and life satisfaction and lowered depressive symptoms over several weeks. Gratitude toward people helps "find, remind and bind" relationships. | Toepfer, Cichy & Peters (2012), *J. Happiness Studies* 13; Algoe (2012), *Soc. & Personality Psych. Compass* 6(6) |
| **Depth over breadth, people over things, surprises** | These are practice recommendations distilled from Emmons's research program, not single trials. | Greater Good Science Center, "Tips for Keeping a Gratitude Journal" |
| **Frequency** | Once a week for six weeks raised happiness. Three times a week did not. | Lyubomirsky, Sheldon & Schkade (2005), *Review of General Psychology* 9(2), as summarized by GGSC |
| **Grateful reappraisal of hard events** | Writing about the positive consequences of an unpleasant open memory gave more closure and less intrusiveness than control writing. | Watkins, Cruz, Holben & Kolts (2008), *J. Positive Psychology* 3(2) |

## How big are the effects?

- **Well-being.** Davis et al. (2016, *J. Counseling Psychology*) found small effects against measurement-only controls, and smaller or no effects against psychologically active controls. In one Dutch RCT with a structured six-week gratitude program, well-being rose clearly at the end (d ≈ 0.6 against an active control) and some of that gain remained six weeks later (d ≈ 0.4). Depression and anxiety did not improve more than in the controls (Bohlmeijer et al., 2021, *J. Happiness Studies*).
- **Depression and anxiety.** Cregg & Cheavens (2021, *J. Happiness Studies*) pooled 27 studies (N = 3,675). They found small reductions (g = −0.29 at the end of the intervention, g = −0.23 at follow-up) and advised people with clinical symptoms to use better-supported treatments.

## What this means for the app

**Starting prompts**, one per best-supported technique:
1. *Something that went well today, and why it happened* (Three Good Things)
2. *Someone who made my day better, and what they did* (people focus)
3. *Something good in my life I might never have had. What if it were gone?* (mental subtraction)

**Library.** It groups prompts by technique, and each prompt shows a one-line reason with its source. You can pick any number of prompts and add your own.

**No streaks or reminders.** The frequency finding suggests daily pressure can backfire.

## Weekly review prompts

The weekly review opens from the calendar and from any Sunday's day view. On Sunday the calendar highlights it. Sunday still has the normal daily prompts too. The reasoning:

- **Weekly is a research-backed pace.** Emmons & McCullough (2003, Study 1) had students write a gratitude list ("up to five things") once a week for 10 weeks. Compared with groups listing hassles or neutral events, they felt better about their lives overall, were more optimistic about the coming week, reported fewer physical symptoms and exercised more (it did not raise positive affect, contrary to what the authors expected). Lyubomirsky and colleagues found that once a week worked better than three times a week, which is a reason to keep a distinct, slower-paced review rather than just more daily prompts.
- **Writing up the week is part of the strengths exercise.** In Seligman et al. (2005), people used a personal strength in a new way each day for a week, then wrote about the strengths they used, what they did, how they felt and what they learned. They were happier and less depressed right afterward, and the happiness gain lasted six months (Greater Good in Action summary).
- **Looking ahead helps too.** "Best Possible Self" writing means describing a detailed, positive future. Student samples reported more positive emotion afterward (Sheldon & Lyubomirsky, 2006; King, 2001), and weekly writing for four weeks raised positive emotions and social connection (Layous et al., 2013). The evidence is mostly from student samples, and benefits were smaller in some groups.

| Weekly prompt | Based on |
| --- | --- |
| The best moments of my week, and why they mattered | Savoring and positive reminiscence (Bryant, 2003) |
| Three good things from this week, and why they happened | Seligman et al. (2005), at a weekly pace |
| Someone who made my week better, and what I want to tell them | Gratitude toward people (Emmons) |
| Up to five things I am grateful for this week | Emmons & McCullough (2003), Study 1 |
| Something I take for granted that I would miss if it were gone | Koo et al. (2008) |
| A strength I used this week, what I did with it, and what I learned | Seligman et al. (2005) |
| Kind things I did or received this week, and how they felt | Lyubomirsky, Sheldon & Schkade (2005) |
| What would make next week a good one? One small thing I will do for it | Best Possible Self (King, 2001; Sheldon & Lyubomirsky, 2006) |
| One thing I am looking forward to next week | Anticipation as savoring |
| A hard thing from this week, and anything good that came out of it | Watkins et al. (2008) |

**Default weekly set (four prompts):** look back (best moments), give thanks (five things), use a strength (what I learned) and look ahead (one small thing for next week). You can pick any number and write your own.

**Not verified in this pass:** the Bryant (2003) savoring prompts, the kindness prompt's "bunched into one day" detail (Lyubomirsky et al., 2005) and Layous et al. (2013) come from the published literature as I remember it, or from a secondary summary. I did not re-read the original papers. The Emmons & McCullough numbers above come from a Psychology Today summary of the study.
